The problem

Forecasting a number is not the same as making a decision

Delhi's winter air kills. The government's response is GRAP — the Graded Response Action Plan — which closes schools, halts construction and restricts vehicles in four escalating stages. Those measures take days to bite. A forecast that arrives the morning the air turns severe is a forecast that arrived too late.

The problem statement asks for a 72-hour forecast that treats pollution and weather as one coupled system. Two things make that hard, and they compound.

Pollution is mostly weather. The same emissions produce a clear day or a catastrophe depending on how deep the air mixes and which way the wind blows. A shallow winter inversion traps a city's exhaust in a few hundred metres of air.

The relationship runs both ways. Aerosol seeds fog. Delhi's worst mornings are pollution and low visibility together, closing runways at IGI and causing pile-ups on the NH-44. A model that only pushes weather into pollution has modelled half the system.

The output that matters is not “PM2.5 will be 340”. It is “there is a 61% chance Stage III is met in 72 hours, driven by upwind fires and a shallow inversion”.

The decision everything rests on

Consume the physics. Don't reimplement it.

The obvious build is a model trained on Delhi's station history. It is also the one that fails, and it fails structurally rather than through bad luck: such a model is autoregressive. It learns how pollution has behaved, not what the atmosphere is going to do. Skill collapses past about 24 hours — exactly where a 72-hour forecast has to work.

CAMS, the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service, publishes a global PM2.5 forecast days ahead, free and keyless. It is coarse — roughly 40 km cells, so all of NCR resolves to 21 of them — and it is biased low over India, because global emission inventories represent India poorly. For 1–3 November 2024 it forecast a mean of 78.7 µg/m³ while Delhi observed several hundred.

That combination is not a defect. It is the opportunity. CAMS supplies the future — winds, transport, regional build-up. We supply the local — the bias correction and the sub-grid detail. And because CAMS is an external, published, internationally used forecast, beating it by a measured margin is a result rather than a claim.

End to end

How a forecast is actually made

Six stages, and the order is the point: each one is only allowed to start once the previous one has answered a yes-or-no question with a number attached.

1

Ingest

One module per source, all with the same shape: fetch(start, end) → DataFrame, written as Parquet partitioned by date, idempotent so a re-run overwrites cleanly. Station gaps are recorded as missing, never interpolated.

2

Align

Every source is joined onto one complete hourly UTC index per station — built before any lag is taken, so shift(24) means 24 hours and not 24 surviving rows. This is where misalignment bugs hide, so it is the most heavily tested code we have.

3

Supervise

One row per (station, issue_time, horizon). Observations are read at or before issue time; forecasts are read at target time, because a forecast for t+72 genuinely is in hand at t. That asymmetry is the whole design — inverting it is leakage.

4

Correct

LightGBM learns the residual between CAMS and observation. One model per (horizon × quantile) — nine small models — so a 72-hour forecast is predicted directly rather than by feeding predictions back in and compounding error.

5

Decide

The predicted distribution is converted into a probability for each GRAP stage, and the city series is modelled directly rather than aggregated from 77 correlated station forecasts after the fact.

6

Serve

FastAPI over the cache. A MapLibre dashboard shows the surface, the stage probabilities, the intervals, the driver attribution, and a “last synced” age that turns red when the data goes stale.

System architecture

Every box, and what is not allowed to touch what

Four layers, one direction of flow. The rule that shapes the whole diagram is that nothing downstream of the store is allowed to make a network call — the models, the evaluation harness, the API and the interface all read cached Parquet. That is what makes the demo survive a CPCB outage, and it is what makes a past week reproducible on demand.

The dashed box matters more than it looks. The evaluation harness is not a reporting afterthought — it is the gate each phase had to pass before the next one was allowed to start, which is why the baseline and the ablation existed before the dashboard did.

The model

Inputs, process, outputs

Inputs

  • CAMS at target hour — 8 species including AOD and dust
  • Observation history — lags at 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72 h; rolling means over 3, 24, 72 h; a 24 h change term
  • Forecast meteorology — 27 fields: wind at 10 m and 100 m as speed, direction and u/v components; boundary-layer height; a ventilation index; lapse rates and an inversion flag
  • Fires — VIIRS/MODIS counts and radiative power over Punjab–Haryana, last 24 and 72 h
  • Upwind corridor — wind-aligned concentration, travel time, advected value now arriving
  • Visibility — measured at VIDP
  • Calendar — hour and day-of-year as sine/cosine pairs, so midnight is adjacent to 23:00

Process

  • Target: the residual — observed minus CAMS — not the concentration itself
  • Nine heads: 3 horizons × 3 quantiles (10th, 50th, 90th), each a separate LightGBM fit with a quantile objective
  • Direct multi-horizon. No recursive rollout anywhere in the codebase
  • Conformal calibration on a split the model never trained on, widening intervals by one number per horizon
  • Graph layer for space: a wind-aware kernel where a northerly makes the northern neighbour count more
  • Exact tree SHAP grouped into human causes for attribution

Outputs

  • PM2.5 at 24 / 48 / 72 h per station, as a 10th–50th–90th percentile band, never a bare point estimate
  • A downscaled surface on a 0.05° grid, every cell carrying its distance to the nearest real monitor
  • GRAP stage probabilities — P(stage ≥ I…IV) with lead time
  • Driver attribution — “upwind fires + shallow inversion”, grouped from SHAP
  • Visibility and fog-risk, as P(visibility < 1 km)

One detail carries more weight than it looks. Every model returns quantiles, not a number. A point forecast of 240 µg/m³ cannot tell a policymaker whether Stage III is in play; a distribution can, and thresholding a distribution is what turns a forecast into a decision.

Staleness is a first-class number, not a detail. The two _runs stores read 2 because we only began archiving live forecast runs this week — and they can never be backfilled.

The interface also carries a caveat we could have hidden. The forecast response includes an input_gap field reporting that the model is trained on archived CAMS and served live CAMS, and that the served input currently runs about 21 µg/m³ low at lead day 1 on the two run-days we have measured. Too few to correct for — so the dashboard says the forecast is uncorrected rather than silently applying a bias fitted on a fortnight of monsoon air.

Replay is the demo centrepiece, and it doubles as validation. Pick any past date and the system rebuilds what it would have forecast from cache alone, then scores it against what CPCB actually recorded. On the 24-hour replay shown in testing: 469 station-hours, model RMSE 30.5 µg/m³ against raw CAMS at 41.3.

Results

Every claim has a baseline next to it

Two baselines appear in every table. Persistence — “tomorrow is like today” — is brutally strong for air quality and published models routinely fail to beat it. Raw CAMS is the physics forecast we claim to improve. A result that does not clear both is a negative result and is reported as one.

The headline, with error bars

A single split is not evidence for effects of a few percent, so every contested claim is re-run over five expanding-window folds, each holding out a whole season. Both models see the same fold, so seasonal variation cancels.

The coupling, proved in the hard direction

Weather-to-pollution is in every air-quality model. The direction the problem statement actually asks about is the other one: does knowing the aerosol load improve a weather forecast? We tested it on visibility, measured independently at the airport.

Raw GFS visibility is 21.3 km RMSE — the physics model is effectively blind to haze. Correcting it with weather alone gives 1.19 km. Adding pollution gives 1.08 km, a 9.5% improvement. And it passes the test that separates physics from a bigger feature set: the gain concentrates where the aerosol is — +0.3% in clean air, +8.9% in poor air. Fog-alarm recall rises from 84% to 91%.

Space, and where it fails

Each station is deleted from the network and predicted from the others — no neighbours of its own, no history of its own. Held-out error is 82.6 µg/m³, against distance-weighted interpolation at 109.6 and raw CAMS at 92.3.

But raw CAMS beats our graph at three of six held-out stations, and they are all the clean peripheral sites — the graph pulls a low-concentration outer station toward the dirtier city its neighbours describe. Against an observed mean of 167 µg/m³, an error of 83 is large. The surface is good enough to say which part of the city is worse this morning. It is not good enough to quote a number for an unmonitored block, and the interface does not pretend otherwise.

The decision layer

From a distribution to a policy call

GRAP is invoked on Delhi's city-wide average AQI, so we model that series directly. Stage thresholds are transcribed from the official CAQM schedule. The declaration threshold falls as severity rises, because the costs are asymmetric: missing a severe episode is far worse than a false alarm, so Stage III is declared at a one-in-four chance while Stage I needs an even one.

We report per-class recall, never overall accuracy. Stage IV is rare — 18 event hours in the test period. A model that predicted “not Stage IV” every single time would score above 99% accuracy and be worthless. And with 18 events, the Stage IV number above is a sample-size artefact, not a capability. We say so rather than headline it.

What we got wrong

The negative results are the credibility

Three of the ideas we were most confident about do not pay for themselves on one year of data. All three are in the results tables under their own headings.

  • The upwind corridor does not yet help. 24 monitors up the Punjab corridor, +0.0% across folds. The transport signal is probably already implicit in the forecast wind field, or timing needs a real trajectory model rather than distance ÷ wind speed.
  • The coupled multi-output model does not beat the single-output one. The likely reason is data, not architecture: observed visibility comes from one airport and is broadcast to every station, so it carries one city-wide signal the meteorology already supplies.
  • Fires help less than the physics says they should — +1.3%, on 3 of 5 folds. But their best fold by far is the stubble season itself, at +4.3%, which is exactly where the mechanism predicts. One burning season cannot separate that from noise.

We were also wrong in a way that is worth admitting because we caught it ourselves. An early single split reported coupling as a clear negative. Rolling-origin evaluation overturned it. That is why nothing in this project is claimed from one split any more.

For each of these the fix is more winters, not more features — and every gain is smaller than its own fold-to-fold spread, so the honest verdict is “no measurable effect either way”, not “it helps”.

How we keep ourselves honest

Eight rules, each one written after something went wrong

RuleWhat it prevents
Never use reanalysis at inferenceERA5 lags real time by ~5 days. Training on it and serving forecasts is a distribution mismatch that inflates every score.
Persistence in every tableThe baseline most air-quality papers quietly omit, because it is hard to beat.
Never random-split a time seriesAdjacent hours leak the answer. We hold out whole episodes with a 96-hour embargo either side.
No recursive rolloutFeeding predictions back in compounds error across 72 hours. One head per horizon instead.
Per-class recall, never accuracyRare severe classes make overall accuracy meaningless.
Gaps are missing, not interpolableCPCB stations go offline and swap instruments. Bridging a gap invents data.
Never claim CAMS's resolution as oursNeighbourhood detail must come from our downscaling and be proved by leave-one-station-out.
Cache everything; never live-query in the demoAn upstream outage must degrade the demo, not kill it.

Some of these came from real failures. Matching stations by distance alone once selected a registration that stopped reporting in 2021 — and empty ground truth looks exactly like a routine outage. Sorting quantiles once moved the baseline's median, turning “raw CAMS” into a bias-corrected CAMS and flattering the very thing we have to beat.

The question we will be asked

“The ministry already funds SAFAR and a Decision Support System. Why you?”

We don't replace the physics model — we correct it. Here is the bias in the operational forecast, here is our correction, here is the RMSE reduction with error bars across five seasons, and the same method applies to your model as easily as it does to CAMS.

That answer only exists because the baseline and the ablation were built before the dashboard. It is also why the negative results stay on the page: a team that reports three things that did not work is a team you can believe about the two that did.
